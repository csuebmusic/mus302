# Module 1 Discussion: Pick a Reflective Question

This is the discussion thread for Module 1. The work happens in two stages: an initial post in which you pick one of the four reflective questions at the end of the Module 1 listening guides and respond to it, and two responses to classmates' posts that Canvas will assign you automatically after the initial-post deadline.

## How it works

Each of the four Module 1 listening guides ends with a reflective question. The questions are:

- **Sam Cooke, "A Change Is Gonna Come" (1964)**: Cooke could have made this song as a gospel song, a blues, or a folk song. He chose to make it with strings and horns, in the language of mid-century pop orchestration. Why? What does that choice say about who he was speaking to and what he was asking of them?
- **Fania All-Stars with Celia Cruz, "Quimbara" (1974, Live in Africa)**: Cruz performed this song in a Kinshasa stadium, on the same weekend James Brown was performing at the same festival, in front of 80,000 people in an African country where her own Afro-Cuban musical roots had originated centuries earlier. How does that context change what you hear, if at all? What is the relationship between music and the place where it is heard? And what do you make of the fact that this homecoming was funded by an authoritarian government that used it for its own political purposes?
- **Sugar Pie DeSanto, "I Don't Wanna Fuss" (1964)**: The song is built on a conversation between DeSanto's voice and the band. Pick one moment in that conversation (a particular horn answer, a shift in the band, a moment where the band changes how it is responding to her). What is the band saying back to her, and how does that response shape what her voice ends up meaning?
- **Hank Williams, "I'm So Lonesome I Could Cry" (1949)**: Williams's phrasing is shaped by the blues, which he learned from a Black musician named Tee Tot. His poetic vocabulary is shaped by older Anglo-Celtic ballad traditions. The song you are hearing is American because both of those traditions met in him. How does knowing this change what you hear, if at all? What do you make of the fact that country music's history is rarely told this way, that the contributions of figures like Tee Tot are often left out of the story even when the music itself carries those contributions forward?

Pick one. Read the listening guide for the track if you have not already, then write your post in response to that track's reflective question. The full questions live in the listening guides themselves, with more context than the summaries above.

## What a strong post looks like

A strong post does four things:

- It picks one specific track and engages with that track's reflective question directly. Quoting back part of the question is fine; your job is to answer it.
- It rests on at least one specific moment in the recording. Timestamps are great. So is a precise description of what is happening in the moment.
- It uses the vocabulary the methodology reading gave you. Timbre, texture, form, gesture. The Description-Pattern-Claim-Evidence move. You do not have to use all of these. You should use the ones that fit your argument.
- It makes a claim someone could disagree with. The reflective questions are designed to invite a position, not a summary.

Two to four paragraphs is the right length. Longer is fine if you have more to say. Shorter is fine if you say it well.

## Responding to classmates

After the initial-post deadline, Canvas will assign you two classmates' posts to respond to. You will not pick them yourself; Canvas's peer-review tool handles assignment automatically. This means you will likely be reading posts on tracks you did not pick, which is part of the design: the thread spreads coverage across all four tracks rather than concentrating on the most popular questions.

Good responses do one or more of these things:

- Engage with the specific moment your classmate pointed to. If you heard it differently, say so. "I heard that moment doing something else, here is what" is welcome.
- Add to the argument by pointing at another moment in the same track that fits or complicates what your classmate said.
- Connect the post to a moment in another track in the listening guide. Cross-track connections are part of the work.
- Ask a question that pushes the argument further. Not "great post!" but something more like: "you said the strings on 'A Change Is Gonna Come' are doing the political work; do you think Cooke's vocal would carry the same weight on a sparer arrangement, or does the orchestral framing matter to what the song means?"

## Logistics

- Initial post: due by Sunday, May 31 (the Module 1 deadline)
- Two assigned responses: due by Sunday, June 7
- Length: two to four paragraphs for the initial post, a paragraph or two for each response
- AI use: allowed with disclosure, per the generative AI policy in the syllabus. If you used AI for this post, end your post with a one-sentence acknowledgment ("I used [tool] to [purpose].")

## Outcomes addressed

This discussion supports SLO 1 (expressing informed opinions and judgments about a variety of styles of popular music), SLO 2 (communicating critical thoughts and judgments about the creative work of different artists), SLO 3 (cultural connections), and SLO 5 (identifying and discussing forms and influences within and across traditions). It also supports the GE-UD-3 outcome on applying the methodologies of the arts and humanities, and the Diversity Overlay outcome on describing the histories and views of cultural groups.
