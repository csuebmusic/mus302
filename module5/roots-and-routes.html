Module 5: European American Immigrant and Working-Class Traditions · Reading

Framing Reading Intro

Like the previous module on Asian American music, this one covers a category whose internal diversity is enormous. People whose grandparents and great-grandparents arrived in the United States at different times, from radically different places, speaking different languages, practicing different religions, and meeting very different American receptions when they arrived are all "European American" by some present-day reckoning. A nineteenth-century farming family in the Tennessee hills, a Jewish family from a shtetl in Russian-controlled Poland setting up on the Lower East Side in 1905, an Italian Catholic family in a Bronx tenement in 1920, and a German-speaking refugee composer fleeing Nazi Europe in 1938 are all "European American" today. They were not all white in the same way at the same time, and they were not always white at all.

That last point is what makes this module different from the others. The communities Module 5 covers were not always understood as a single "European American" or "white" group. Irish Catholics arriving in the 1840s, Italian and Polish Catholics arriving in the 1880s and 1890s, Jewish immigrants from the Russian Empire arriving between 1881 and 1924: each of these groups was, at the time of arrival, racialized as something other than white in the dominant American racial logic of the period. The consolidation of these groups into a single "Caucasian" category happened gradually, through specific political and legal events, over the same decades the music this module covers was being made. The whiteness this module covers is an outcome these communities arrived at, and the music was made on the way there rather than from inside it.

The traditions this module covers are distinct. The Scots-Irish ballad and dance music tradition that became Appalachian folk and then country and bluegrass; the Eastern European Jewish songwriting tradition that became Tin Pan Alley, Broadway, and the ; the Italian, Irish, Polish, and other working-class Catholic traditions that flowed through urban , , and the of the late 1970s; and the central and Eastern European tradition of classical training that, transplanted to the United States by mid-century refugees and their American-born students, helped produce the -based electronic music that reshaped American popular sound from the late 1960s onward. What these traditions share is the long, often-uncomfortable American process by which their carriers came to be understood as "white," and a sustained, often-unequal dialogue with African American music that runs through every one of them. Module 5 is the cultural-roots module where the category itself is hardest to hold steady, and the listening guides take that instability as a feature rather than a problem.

Whiteness as a process

The idea that "white" is a single stable racial category covering all Americans of European descent is a relatively recent one, and historians have spent the past several decades carefully taking it apart. The most influential single book on this is Matthew Frye Jacobson's Whiteness of a Different Color: European Immigrants and the Alchemy of Race, published in 1998, which traces what Jacobson calls a "political history of whiteness" through three periods. From 1790 to 1840, the United States naturalization law restricted citizenship to "free white persons" and that category was understood, in Jacobson's reading, to mean essentially Anglo-Saxons, with everyone else either excluded or grudgingly accepted. From 1840 to 1924, mass immigration from Ireland, Germany, Italy, Greece, Russia, Poland, and elsewhere fractured the category: the dominant nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century scientific racism divided Europeans into competing "races" (Anglo-Saxon, Celtic, Mediterranean, Slavic, Hebrew, Alpine, Nordic) arranged in a hierarchy, with Anglo-Saxons on top. From 1924 forward, after the National Origins Act drastically restricted immigration from southern and eastern Europe, the various European "races" were gradually reconsolidated into the single "Caucasian" category that twentieth-century Americans came to take for granted.

This is not a neat story. It does not say that any group of European immigrants was ever treated as Black people were treated; the racial regime of slavery, segregation, and anti-Black violence operated on a different and more brutal axis. Even the most embattled European immigrant groups had legal access to citizenship, to property, to public space, and to mobility that Black Americans were systematically denied. What the whiteness-as-process scholarship establishes is that among Americans understood as not-Black, the racial category was unstable and politically constructed, and the immigrants the term "European American" now covers came in at different rungs and rose at different speeds. Irish Catholics arriving during the famine years of the 1840s were depicted in nativist political cartoons as racial inferiors, often drawn with simian features, and were targets of mob violence (the 1844 anti-Catholic riots in Philadelphia, the 1855 Bloody Monday riots in Louisville, anti-Irish hiring discrimination throughout the second half of the century). Italian immigrants in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries faced a similar racialization, including anti-Italian mob violence in New Orleans, Tampa, and other cities, alongside widespread employment, housing, and educational discrimination. Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe faced anti-Semitic exclusion from hotels, neighborhoods, professions, and universities through the middle of the twentieth century.

The point of laying this out is not to flatten distinctions between groups or to argue that European immigrant suffering matched the structural racism leveled at Black Americans, peoples, or Asian Americans (it did not). The point is that the people who made the music this module covers were, at the time they were making it, often racialized in ways the present-day "European American" frame does not capture. When and the Teenagers recorded "Why Do Fools Fall in Love" in 1955, the group's two Puerto Rican members were navigating a racial situation that would not have allowed them to call themselves white in any straightforward way, and the group's Italian American audience-base in mid-century New York was itself less than fully white in the racial logic that had shaped the city a generation earlier. When Carole King and Gerry Goffin wrote "Up on the Roof" for the Drifters in 1962, they were Jewish songwriters working in an industry that, two decades earlier, had still operated under explicit anti-Semitic exclusions in adjacent industries (banking, law, medicine). When Bruce Springsteen wrote "The River" in 1979 and 1980, he was writing as the American-born son of an Irish-Italian Catholic family from Freehold, New Jersey, working from a tradition of white working-class identity that depended in part on the postwar GI Bill, the Federal Housing Administration's policies, and the union-shop industrial economy of the Northeast and Midwest, all of which had functioned to consolidate the various European-immigrant groups into a single "white" working class while explicitly excluding Black Americans from the same benefits. The whiteness this module covers is, in every case, a whiteness with a date and a route.

Four threads

The five anchor tracks in this module trace four distinct threads through the European American story. The threads overlap and cross-pollinate, but each has its own migration history, its own institutional infrastructure, and its own dialogue with the rest of the American musical landscape.

The Scots-Irish ballad tradition

Beginning in the early eighteenth century and accelerating through the middle of that century, large numbers of Protestant settlers from the lowlands of Scotland and the north of Ireland (the population usually called "Scots-Irish" or "Ulster Scots" in the United States, "Ulster Scots" in the UK) crossed the Atlantic and moved south and west through Pennsylvania into the Appalachian backcountry. By the time of the American Revolution, the Scots-Irish were one of the largest non-English white populations in the colonies, and they were heavily concentrated in what would become West Virginia, eastern Kentucky, eastern Tennessee, southwestern Virginia, and western North Carolina, the region the twentieth century came to call . They brought with them an oral ballad tradition that ran back through Scotland and the north of England and that included narratives of murder, betrayal, exile, supernatural visitation, and aristocratic catastrophe; they brought dance music, unaccompanied hymn singing, and the basic instrumental and vocal grammars that would, over the next two centuries, be reshaped into Appalachian old-time music, , and bluegrass.

The "ballad-hunting" expeditions of the early twentieth century, conducted by figures like Cecil Sharp (working in Appalachia in 1916, 1917, and 1918 with Olive Dame Campbell and his protégé Maud Karpeles), produced a substantial scholarly record of the Scots-Irish-derived material that had survived, sometimes in forms older than what could still be heard in the British Isles. Sharp and his collaborators leaned on the ballad collection compiled by the Harvard professor Francis James Child, whose English and Scottish Popular Ballads (published in five volumes from 1882 to 1898) catalogued 305 narrative ballads with their textual variants; ballads that survived in Appalachia were and are referred to as "Child ballads" with their Child catalogue numbers. Sharp's published collections, the most important of which is English Folk Songs from the Southern Appalachians (1917, expanded 1932), gave the early-twentieth-century American folk movement and, later, the 1960s folk revival a shared canon to draw from. The track this module anchors on the Scots-Irish thread (Joan Baez singing "Mary Hamilton," Child ballad 173, on her 1960 self-titled debut album) is a folk-revival recording reaching back through Sharp's collections to the Scottish source.

The Sharp narrative also has problems, and recent scholarship has pushed hard against parts of it. Sharp consistently undercounted the Scottish (versus English) origins of the material he collected, and his project was substantially shaped by his guides' decisions to lead him toward white-identified mountain communities while the Black, Indigenous, and multiracial musical communities of the same region went under-documented. The banjo, central to the Appalachian sound by Sharp's time, was an instrument of African origin reconstructed from West African prototypes by enslaved Black musicians, then taken up by white minstrel performers from the 1830s onward and later by white Appalachian musicians who often had no idea of its history. The Appalachian musical tradition the Scots-Irish narrative wraps up neatly was always, in fact, a hybrid, and "Mary Hamilton" appears in this module as one strand inside a larger weave rather than the whole tapestry.

The Eastern European Jewish songwriting tradition

Between approximately 1881 and 1924, more than two million Jewish immigrants left the Russian Empire, Russian-controlled Poland, Ukraine, Lithuania, and adjacent regions for the United States, driven by waves of state-sanctioned anti-Jewish violence () that began in earnest after the 1881 assassination of Czar Alexander II and continued into the 1920s, and by the broader poverty and legal restriction Jewish life in the Russian Empire's Pale of Settlement entailed. Most of these immigrants settled initially in New York City, with the largest concentration on the Lower East Side of Manhattan. Their American-born and immigrant children entered the entertainment industries in disproportionate numbers, partly because so many other industries (banking, law, medicine, academia, much of corporate America) were closed to Jews by formal or informal anti-Semitic exclusion through the 1930s and 1940s, and partly because the entertainment industries (, publishing, the new film business, the new recording business) were themselves new enough to be open to outsiders.

The first institutional home for the music this immigrant generation made was not Tin Pan Alley but the . The first Yiddish theatrical performance in the United States was given on August 12, 1882, at 66 East 4th Street in lower Manhattan, six years after Abraham Goldfaden founded the first professional Yiddish theater company in Romania. By 1900 New York City was the world center of Yiddish theater. By 1925 the strip of Second Avenue running south from 14th Street, in what is now the East Village, housed more than a dozen Yiddish-language theaters and was known as the "Yiddish Broadway" or "Yiddish Rialto"; at its peak the district drew hundreds of thousands of theatergoers a year, sustained Yiddish-language press and radio infrastructure, and supported a robust ecosystem of cafes, music stores, sheet-music publishers, and the first theatrical union in the United States (the Hebrew Actors Union, founded in 1899). The repertoire combined translated European drama and operetta with original Yiddish-language musicals built around the four major Second Avenue composers (Joseph Rumshinsky, Abraham Ellstein, Sholom Secunda, and Alexander Olshanetsky), most of whom drew their early musical training from (the sung liturgical tradition of the synagogue) before moving into theatrical composition. Both and grew up inside this district. Berlin's father was a cantor; the Gershwin family lived for years at 91 Second Avenue, in the heart of the Yiddish Theatre District, and the brothers regularly attended the local Yiddish theaters as children. The musical sensibilities of the next generation of Tin Pan Alley songwriters were not abstract products of immigration; they came out of a specific neighborhood, a specific theatrical tradition, and a specific set of synagogue and stage musical models the children grew up hearing.

The corner of West 28th Street between Fifth and Sixth Avenues in Manhattan, where most of the major sheet-music publishing houses concentrated in the 1890s and early 1900s, came to be called , and the term came to mean both that physical block and the larger sheet-music-driven popular song industry of which it was the center. Irving Berlin (born Israel Beilin on May 11, 1888, somewhere in the Russian Empire, arrived on the Lower East Side at age five with his family in 1893) and George Gershwin (born Jacob Gershowitz on September 26, 1898, in Brooklyn to Russian Jewish immigrants from Vilnius and St. Petersburg) became the two best-known figures of a Tin Pan Alley songwriting generation that included Jerome Kern, Harold Arlen (born Hyman Arluck, the son of a cantor), Harry Von Tilzer (born Aaron Gumbinsky), Doc Pomus, the Gershwin brothers' frequent collaborator Yip Harburg, Sammy Cahn, and a long list of others, almost all of them Jewish sons and daughters of Eastern European immigrants. The composer Cole Porter, the rare non-Jewish songwriter who broke through into the same commercial tier, told Richard Rodgers his secret for writing successful "American" music was to "write Jewish tunes." As Rachel Rubin and Jeffrey Melnick argue in Immigration and American Popular Culture, the Tin Pan Alley story is impossible to tell without the Jewish-immigrant component, and impossible to tell honestly without acknowledging that the Tin Pan Alley songbook was constantly drawing on Black American musical material, sometimes in straightforward exchange and sometimes through the framework of that had dominated American popular entertainment through the nineteenth century. Berlin's "Alexander's Band" (1911) and Gershwin's "Swanee" (1919, made famous by Al Jolson) made their authors household names by relying on audiences hearing the songs as recognizably Black; Jolson's 1927 film The Jazz Singer, the first feature-length film with synchronized dialogue sequences, told a story about a Jewish cantor's son rebelling against his father's tradition by performing American popular music in blackface, a story the film's broadly Jewish audience and its broadly white American audience read as triumphant assimilation but which a present-day viewer reads as something more uncomfortable. Jeffrey Melnick's A Right to Sing the Blues (1999) is the standard scholarly account of how the broader Tin Pan Alley exchange worked.

Tin Pan Alley as a sheet-music-publishing industry began to give way in the late 1920s to a new institutional center, the Broadway musical theater. The pivot is usually located at Show Boat, the Kern and Hammerstein adaptation of Edna Ferber's 1926 novel that opened at the Ziegfeld Theatre on December 27, 1927 (less than three months after The Jazz Singer premiered on October 6). Up to Show Boat, Broadway musicals had largely been revues, operettas, and vaudeville-derived assemblages in which the songs were the point and the plot was a holding container; Show Boat integrated music into a serious narrative about race, miscegenation law, alcoholism, and family rupture, with songs that grew out of character and situation rather than serving as standalone numbers. The work is uncomfortable in ways that twenty-first-century revivals have had to confront and rewrite (the original opening chorus contained the n-word; the Black characters were originally written for white actors in blackface in some early productions; the musical's racial politics are at once unprecedented and condescending), and it is at the same time the work that made it possible for the next generation of Broadway composers to try the integrated form. The second pivot, the one that established the Rodgers-and-Hammerstein integrated-musical formula as the Broadway standard, came on March 31, 1943, with the premiere of Oklahoma! at the St. James Theatre, the first collaboration of (who had previously worked with Lorenz Hart) and (Kern's Show Boat collaborator). Oklahoma! ran for 2,212 performances, won a special Pulitzer Prize in 1944, and became the model for the postwar Broadway musical. The Rodgers and Hammerstein run that followed (Carousel 1945, South Pacific 1949, The King and I 1951, The Sound of Music 1959) defined Broadway through the 1950s and produced the bulk of what later came to be called the , a body of work jazz singers, jazz instrumentalists, and pop singers from Frank Sinatra and Ella Fitzgerald forward have been performing and reinterpreting for the eight decades since.

The Brill Building era of the late 1950s and early 1960s, in which a new generation of mostly young, mostly Jewish songwriters working in cubicle-like offices at 1619 Broadway and the nearby 1650 Broadway wrote singles for Black and white vocal groups and singers, is the direct descendant of the Tin Pan Alley and Broadway lines together. The principal institutional engine was Aldon Music, founded by Don Kirshner and Al Nevins in May 1958 and housed at 1650 Broadway across the street from the Brill Building proper, which at its peak employed eighteen songwriters working in piano-equipped cubicles. Carole King (born Carol Joan Klein in Brooklyn in 1942) and Gerry Goffin (born in Brooklyn in 1939), Doc Pomus (born Jerome Solon Felder in Brooklyn in 1925) and Mort Shuman, Jeff Barry and Ellie Greenwich, Phil Spector, Barry Mann and Cynthia Weil, Burt Bacharach and Hal David, Neil Sedaka and Howard Greenfield: nearly all the major Brill Building songwriters were Jewish New Yorkers writing in a tradition that ran back through Berlin and Gershwin to the cantorial and Yiddish-theater music of their grandparents' generation. The track this module anchors on the Brill Building thread ( recording of Goffin and King's "Up on the Roof," released in late 1962) sits squarely in that lineage.

Italian, Irish, and Polish urban Catholic working-class traditions

The third thread runs through the urban Catholic working-class neighborhoods that the late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century mass migrations from southern and eastern Europe produced in the cities of the Northeast and Midwest. Italian immigration peaked between 1880 and 1924 (about four million people in that span, mostly from southern Italy and Sicily); Irish Catholic immigration ran in earlier waves, peaking with the famine years of the 1840s and continuing through the rest of the nineteenth century; Polish, Czech, Slovak, Hungarian, and other central and eastern European Catholic immigration also peaked in the 1880-1924 window. The neighborhoods these immigrants settled in (Brooklyn and the Bronx in New York; the South Side of Chicago; South Philadelphia; the working-class wards of Detroit, Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Buffalo, Newark, and Jersey City) became the settings for a long American working-class musical tradition that ran from the nineteenth-century brass-band and music of immigrant social clubs through twentieth-century crooner-tradition popular song, through the 1950s street-corner doo-wop of mixed urban groups, through 1960s rock and roll, through the 1970s heartland rock of Bruce Springsteen and others, and forward into urban , which is itself a story about Black, Latin, and white working-class urban kids in the same New York streets in the 1970s.

The doo-wop subgenre, in particular, sits at the center of this thread. Mid-1950s doo-wop emerged from the racially mixed working-class urban neighborhoods of New York City, Philadelphia, and other Northeastern cities, and the groups who recorded it were often integrated in ways that had no parallel in mainstream American life of the same period. Frankie Lymon and the Teenagers, the group on Track 1 of this module, included three Black members and two Puerto Rican members; Dion and the Belmonts (Italian American Bronx); the Teenagers' Harlem peers and the Italian American Brooklyn and Bronx singers traded vocal arrangements, harmony approaches, and street-corner audiences across what was, in mid-1950s mainstream American culture, supposedly a hard color line. The doo-wop story is, at one and the same time, a Black music story and a Puerto Rican music story (which Module 3 carries) and an Italian, Irish, and Polish American urban working-class story; this module picks up the last of those threads while keeping the first two visible.

The Bruce Springsteen track that closes this module (the title track of his 1980 double album The River) belongs to this thread. Springsteen, born in 1949 in Long Branch, New Jersey, raised in Freehold, is the American-born son of an Irish and Dutch father (Douglas Springsteen, called "Dutch" for his surname despite being mostly of Irish descent) and an Italian American mother (Adele Zerilli, whose father came from Vico Equense near Naples). He attended the St. Rose of Lima parochial school in Freehold, was raised Catholic, and has consistently identified himself as an Irish-Italian-American Catholic from a working-class Jersey background. The lyrics of "The River" tell the story of an early marriage forced by an unplanned pregnancy and the slow erosion of working-class economic possibility in the late 1970s, and they were, by Springsteen's own account, drawn directly from his older sister Ginny's life. The song stands, in this module, both for the urban Catholic working-class thread and for what happened to that thread when the postwar industrial economy that had made it possible began to come apart in the late 1970s and early 1980s.

The synthesizer pioneers

The fourth thread is harder to gather under a single ethnic-immigration frame than the other three are, and that is itself part of what makes it useful for Module 5. Through the middle of the twentieth century, the United States became the world center of electronic music research and production, partly because of the wartime and postwar migration of European musicians, engineers, and physicists to American universities and industrial-research labs, and partly because of a homegrown American tradition of tinkerer-engineer music-making that ran from Thaddeus Cahill's 1897 Telharmonium through Leon Theremin's 1928 patent to John Cage's 1939 Imaginary Landscape No. 1. By the mid-1960s, the engineer Robert Moog (German-Jewish ancestry, born in New York in 1934) was building the first commercially viable voltage-controlled modular synthesizers in his shop in Trumansburg, New York; the engineer Don Buchla was building parallel instruments on the West Coast; Vladimir Ussachevsky and Otto Luening were running the Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center; Milton Babbitt was working with the RCA Mark II synthesizer at the same center.

The synthesizer figure this module anchors on, Wendy Carlos, was born in Pawtucket, Rhode Island in 1939, studied music and physics at Brown University, and went to Columbia for graduate work in composition with Ussachevsky in the early 1960s. Carlos's debut album, Switched-On Bach, released by in October 1968, did three things at once that none of the prior American electronic-music tradition had done together. It made fully realized recordings of canonical Western classical works (selections by Bach) on the Moog synthesizer at a level of finish that demonstrated, for the first time at this scale, that the new instrument could produce real performances of real music rather than abstract sound effects; it sold to a popular audience in numbers no electronic-music recording had ever approached, eventually being certified platinum and topping the Billboard Classical chart for three years; and it functioned, in Robert Moog's own later account, as the demonstration that opened the door for synthesizers to enter rock, pop, R&B, soul, disco, and every subsequent popular-music genre that took them up. The track this module anchors on the synthesizer thread is the closing piece of Switched-On Bach, Carlos's electronic realization of Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 3 in G major.

Carlos came out as a transgender woman in a 1979 interview, and reissues of her recordings after that point have credited her as Wendy Carlos rather than the Walter Carlos under whom Switched-On Bach was originally released. She is one of the most consequential trans figures in twentieth-century American music. Her own statements over the years have asked, consistently and clearly, that the music be the center of any serious account of her work and that her transition not be made into the story; this module follows that wish. Carlos's significance in the musical-technology history of American popular music is enormous on its own terms, and the listening guide for Track 4 stays mostly on the music.

Where country and folk sit in this module

Country music is, by any reasonable definition, one of the major European American popular traditions. It is also already anchored in this course at Module 1, where 's "I'm So Lonesome I Could Cry" (1949) carries the country thread for the methodology reading. Module 5 picks up the Scots-Irish ballad tradition Williams's lineage descends from, but it does so through the 1960s folk revival rather than through commercial country directly. The folk revival is itself part of the European American story this module covers (Joan Baez's parents were a Mexican-born physicist and a Scottish American social worker; the broader revival's central figures included a New York Jewish son of a leftist family named Robert Zimmerman who renamed himself ), and the reactivation of Scots-Irish balladry through urban Northeastern singers in 1959, 1960, and 1961 is one of the cleaner demonstrations of how a European American tradition can be received, transformed, and politicized across generations. Students interested in commercial country (Williams, Loretta Lynn, Merle Haggard, Dolly Parton, Garth Brooks, contemporary Nashville) have plenty of material for the final project; this module's pass at country is its Scots-Irish ancestor.

Where Black music sits in this story

Every European American popular tradition this module covers is, on close inspection, a tradition shaped at every stage by African American music. The Appalachian banjo is West African in origin. The Tin Pan Alley songbook is structurally indebted to ragtime, , and the , often in ways its Jewish American songwriters were direct about and proud of. Brill Building pop wrote for Black vocal groups (the Drifters, the Shirelles, the Cookies, Little Eva) and learned from them. Doo-wop is itself a Black-led genre that mixed-race urban groups joined as part of a larger tradition that began in Black and vocal practice. The white working-class heartland rock of Springsteen, John Mellencamp, Bob Seger, and others draws from the same R&B, , and gospel traditions Module 2 covers, and Springsteen has consistently named that lineage in interviews and on stage. The synthesizer tradition is the partial exception, and even there the post-1968 popular adoption of the synthesizer was led by Black artists (Stevie Wonder's run from Music of My Mind in 1972 forward, Sly and the Family Stone's There's a Riot Goin' On in 1971, Herbie Hancock's Head Hunters in 1973, the Detroit techno pioneers Juan Atkins, Derrick May, and Kevin Saunderson in the 1980s) more than by white artists, and the listening guides for both Carlos and the Brill Building track make this dialogue explicit.

The point is not that European American music is "really" Black music, or that the European American carriers of these traditions were merely passing along material that was not theirs. The point is that the four threads above and the broader American popular tradition they sit inside are products of a sustained, often-unequal exchange between European immigrant communities and African American communities, and that an honest framing of "European American" music has to keep that exchange in view from the start.

The arc of the anchor tracks

The five anchor tracks for this module run chronologically and trace a specific path through the European American popular landscape between 1956 and 1980. Frankie Lymon and the Teenagers' "Why Do Fools Fall in Love" (Track 1, 1956) opens the module on cross-ethnic urban doo-wop in mid-century New York, with a group whose membership crosses Black, Puerto Rican, and (through the doo-wop scene's audiences and adjacent groups) Italian American lines. Joan Baez's "Mary Hamilton" (Track 2, 1960) pulls all the way back to the Scots-Irish ballad inheritance of Appalachia, recorded by a 19-year-old Mexican-Scottish folk-revival singer in a Manhattan ballroom. The Drifters' "Up on the Roof" (Track 3, 1962) pivots forward to the Brill Building's Jewish American songwriting institution and the urban-tenement lyric content the song carries, sung by an African American vocal group with Goffin and King's words. Wendy Carlos's "Switched-On Bach" Brandenburg Concerto No. 3 excerpt (Track 4, 1968) breaks the singer-songwriter assumption Tracks 2 and 3 share and points toward what synthesis would do for the next half-century of American music. Bruce Springsteen's "The River" (Track 5, 1980) closes the module on the white urban Catholic working-class thread at the moment of its early-Reagan-era political crisis. The path runs from cross-ethnic urban exchange through the Scots-Irish ballad source through the Brill Building songwriter system through electronic synthesis through deindustrialization-era heartland rock, with the constant dialogue with African American music audible at every step.

What this module does not cover

Several large and important European American popular traditions are not represented on the anchor tracks, by necessity rather than choice. , the German and central European dance music that became a major American working-class tradition through the early and middle twentieth century (and which had its own commercial recording industry, its own radio circuit, and its own substantial postwar audience among Polish, German, Czech, and Slovak Americans), does not appear on an anchor track here. The German and Eastern European Jewish art-music tradition that came across with the wave of refugees fleeing Nazi Europe in the 1930s (Arnold Schoenberg, Kurt Weill, Erich Korngold, Hanns Eisler, Ernst Krenek, and others) reshaped twentieth-century American film music, art music, and musical theater in profound ways and is not here. The Italian American crooner tradition that ran from Frank Sinatra and Tony Bennett through Dean Martin and Bobby Darin is here only sideways, through Track 5's adjacency: Springsteen has consistently named Sinatra as a foundational figure for his own Italian American Jersey vocal sensibility, and the heartland-rock track carries the postwar Italian American working-class voice the crooners established forward into the 1970s and 1980s. rock of the 1970s, which the Carnegie Hall Migrations festival materials and the broader scholarship treat as a working-class urban European American genre with deep ties to the same neighborhoods Springsteen came out of, is not on an anchor track; the Ramones, Patti Smith, X, the Clash's American reception, and the long arc that runs into 1980s American hardcore would all reward a final project. in the 1990s, the broader 1990s indie-rock scene, the European American side of , and the contemporary genre are also possibilities for the final project.

The lineup this module follows is one path through European American music. It is not the path. The student final project is the place to follow the trajectory somewhere this module did not go.

Pareles's overview of the Carnegie Hall Migrations: The Making of America festival, which framed the Scots-Irish, Eastern European Jewish, and Great Migration threads in parallel.

Carnegie Hall Weill Music Institute. Migrations: The Making of America festival brochure, 2018-2019. Programming materials for the citywide Carnegie Hall festival on immigrant musical legacies; consulted for framing of the three migrations and their American musical descendants.

Niebur, Louis. "Switched-On Bach: Wendy Carlos." Library of Congress National Recording Registry essay, 2005. The Library of Congress's official essay on the album, source for production details and the reception history.

Pinch, Trevor, and Frank Trocco. Analog Days: The Invention and Impact of the Moog Synthesizer. Harvard University Press, 2002. The standard history of the Moog synthesizer's development and early reception, with substantial attention to Carlos's role.

Myers, John E. "Influence of immigrants on American music." Music Research Starters, EBSCO, 2024. A general reference overview of the topic; consulted for the long-range structuring of the European immigrant threads.
