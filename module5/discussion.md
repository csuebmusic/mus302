# Module 5 Discussion: Pick a Reflective Question

This is the discussion thread for Module 5. The work happens in two stages: an initial post in which you pick one of the five reflective questions at the end of the Module 5 listening guides and respond to it, and two responses to classmates' posts that Canvas will assign you automatically after the initial-post deadline.

## How it works

Each of the five Module 5 listening guides ends with a reflective question. The questions are:

- **Frankie Lymon and the Teenagers, "Why Do Fools Fall in Love" (1956)**: The framing reading argued that doo-wop in mid-1950s New York was, structurally, an integrated music, because the urban neighborhoods it was made in had Black, Puerto Rican, Italian American, and Jewish American teenagers on the same streets. Pick one specific moment in the recording (Sherman Garnes's bass-singer "oo-wah" intro, Lymon's first entrance, the harmony group sitting behind him on the title line of the chorus, Jimmy Wright's saxophone solo, the bridge where Lymon's voice strains slightly at the top of his range, the bass-singer return at the end) and make an argument for what that moment is doing musically and what it is doing socially. What is the moment teaching a 1956 listener about how a song can sound, and what is it teaching the same listener, less directly, about who can sing together and who can be heard together? Does the political work of the moment depend on the listener knowing in advance who the five singers are, or does the sound itself carry the argument?
- **Joan Baez, "Mary Hamilton" (1960)**: Take a position on one of two questions and defend it from specific moments in the recording. First: does the restraint of Baez's delivery serve the ballad, or does it hold the listener at a distance the ballad's emotional content does not deserve? Cite at least one specific verse or moment as evidence. Second: what would have changed if Baez had anchored her debut album on a different inheritance (the corridos and rancheras of her father's Mexican family, or contemporary topical songwriting in the mode Bob Dylan was about to introduce, or the gospel and rhythm-and-blues she could have heard on the radio in 1960)? What kind of folk-revival singer would she have become, and what would the 1960 album have lost or gained?
- **The Drifters, "Up on the Roof" (1962)**: The framing reading argued that the Brill Building system was structurally racist in a specific way: a mostly-white, disproportionately Jewish American songwriter cadre wrote some of the most enduring American popular songs of the twentieth century, and a mostly-Black vocal-group cadre made those songs famous, with the writers and publishers retaining most of the rights and most of the income. Pick one specific moment in the recording (the opening string entrance, Rudy Lewis's first vocal phrase, the kettledrum's first appearance, the suspended chord at the end of a verse, the falsetto "up on the roof" tag after a chorus line, the final repeated chorus where strings and vocal harmony swell together) and make an argument for what that moment is doing musically and what it is doing socially. What is the moment teaching a 1962 listener about how a pop record can sound, and what is it teaching the same listener, less directly, about who is allowed to write a popular song and who is allowed to be heard singing one? Does it change your hearing of the moment to know that the singer was paid a weekly wage and the writers retained the royalties?
- **Wendy Carlos, *Switched-On Bach*, Third Brandenburg Concerto, first movement (1968)**: Pick one specific moment in Carlos's recording (the opening galloping figure of the first movement, the staggered entries of the three violin voices about thirty seconds in, the two-chord Phrygian cadence at the center of the piece, the moment at which the three cellos answer the violins in the third movement, the final tutti close on the G-major cadence) and make an argument for what that moment is doing musically and what it is doing historically. What is the moment teaching a 1968 listener about what a synthesizer can sound like, and about how European art-music can travel into American popular music? Does it change your hearing of the moment to know that there is no live ensemble, that what you hear is one keyboardist's year of layered overdubs on a single custom-built instrument in a Manhattan apartment, and that the audience for the album included a million people who had not previously bought a classical record?
- **Bruce Springsteen, "The River" (1980)**: Pick one specific moment in the song (the opening eighteen seconds of harmonica and acoustic guitar before the voice enters, the dropped-out arrangement under the third verse's "I remember us riding in my brother's car," the held G that opens the first chorus, the wordless harmony "ooh" responses on the long outro, the final harmonica phrase that closes the recording without any further vocal). Make an argument for what that moment is doing musically, and answer one of two questions about it. First: does it change your hearing of the moment to know that the narrator is Springsteen's brother-in-law rather than Springsteen himself, and that the song narrates a marriage that, contrary to its own implied trajectory, has lasted more than fifty years? Second: which of the cultural-roots threads running through the song (Italian American crooner phrasing, doo-wop and R&B vocal vocabulary, Hank Williams country songwriting, the dialogue with African American music that runs through all of those) is most audible at this moment, and what is it doing there?

Pick one. Read the listening guide for the track if you have not already, then write your post in response to that track's reflective question. The full questions live in the listening guides themselves, with more context than the summaries above.

## What a strong post looks like

A strong post does four things:

- It picks one specific track and engages with that track's reflective question directly. Quoting back part of the question is fine; your job is to answer it.
- It rests on at least one specific moment in the recording. Timestamps are great. So is a precise description of what is happening in the moment.
- It uses the vocabulary the readings have given you. Timbre, texture, form, gesture; whiteness as a process; the framing reading's account of the four threads and their continuous dialogue with each other and with African American music; the Brill Building system; the Italian American crooner tradition; cross-harp position; the country songwriting tradition Hank Williams anchored on Module 1. You do not have to use all of these. You should use the ones that fit your argument.
- It makes a claim someone could disagree with. The reflective questions are designed to invite a position, not a summary.

Two to four paragraphs is the right length. Longer is fine if you have more to say. Shorter is fine if you say it well.

## Responding to classmates

After the initial-post deadline, Canvas will assign you two classmates' posts to respond to. You will not pick them yourself; Canvas's peer-review tool handles assignment automatically. This means you will likely be reading posts on tracks you did not pick, which is part of the design: the thread spreads coverage across all five tracks rather than concentrating on the most popular questions.

Good responses do one or more of these things:

- Engage with the specific moment your classmate pointed to. If you heard it differently, say so. "I heard that moment doing something else, here is what" is welcome.
- Add to the argument by pointing at a moment in the same track that fits or complicates what your classmate said.
- Connect the post to a moment in another Module 5 track or in earlier modules. Cross-track connections are part of the work in this module specifically: the Lymon and Springsteen tracks bookend the same thread a generation apart, the Drifters and Springsteen tracks both run on Goffin-King and Brill Building songwriting inheritances, and the Hank Williams country thread reaches both the Baez and Springsteen tracks.
- Ask a question that pushes the argument further. Not "great post!" but something more like: "you argued that the Brill Building's structural racism is audible in Rudy Lewis's restrained delivery; do you think the same restraint reads as structural constraint, or as artistic choice, or both, and how would we tell the difference from the recording?"

## Logistics

- Initial post: due by Sunday, June 28 (the Module 5 deadline)
- Two assigned responses: due by Sunday, July 5
- Length: two to four paragraphs for the initial post, a paragraph or two for each response
- AI use: allowed with disclosure, per the generative AI policy in the syllabus. If you used AI for this post, end your post with a one-sentence acknowledgment ("I used [tool] to [purpose].")

## Outcomes addressed

This discussion supports SLO 2 (communicating critical thoughts and judgments about the creative work of different artists), SLO 3 (cultural connections between music and social, political, and economic context), and SLO 5 (identifying and discussing forms and influences within and across traditions). It also supports the GE-UD-3 outcome on applying the methodologies of the arts and humanities, and the Diversity Overlay outcomes on describing the histories and views of cultural groups (the four threads the module covers) and on the intersection of social identities (the Lymon track foregrounds race and ethnicity and class together; the Drifters track foregrounds the structural intersection of race, religion, and labor relations inside the Brill Building system; the Springsteen track foregrounds the intersection of race, class, and the long postwar consolidation of European-immigrant identity into a single "white" category).
