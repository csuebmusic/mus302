# Module 5 Checkpoint Quiz

A short quiz covering the framing reading and the five anchor tracks of Module 5. Twelve questions, multiple choice, low-stakes. Students may retake the quiz as many times as they want; highest score counts.

The structured version below is for reading. The "PASTE INTO CANVAS" block further down has each question in plain text, ready to paste one at a time when building the Canvas quiz.

## Quiz introduction (paste into the Canvas quiz description box)

Welcome to the Module 5 Checkpoint Quiz. Like the earlier module quizzes, this one is short and low-stakes. It exists to make sure the framing reading and the five anchor tracks have landed before you move on.

A few things to know before you start:

There are 12 multiple-choice questions. Plan for about 15 to 20 minutes, though you can take as long as you need. There is no time limit.

You can retake this quiz as many times as you want. Your highest score is the one that counts. If you miss a question, go back to the relevant reading or listening guide, then come back and try again.

The questions cover three things: the framing reading's vocabulary and arguments (Matthew Frye Jacobson's three-period account of American whiteness, the National Origins Act of 1924, the four threads the module covers, the postwar consolidation through the GI Bill and FHA mortgage system, the Brill Building system, and the continuous dialogue with African American music that runs through every thread), specific facts and contexts from the five anchor tracks (Lymon, Baez, the Drifters, Carlos, Springsteen), and the methodology vocabulary from earlier modules applied to the new tracks (timbre, texture, form, gesture).

You should complete the framing reading and all five listening guides before taking this quiz. The quiz is the last thing you do in Module 5, after the discussion post.

If something on the quiz seems unclear or you think a question is wrong, email me. I would rather hear about it than have you stuck.

Good luck.

## Questions (structured for reading)

### 1.

The framing reading anchors its argument on Matthew Frye Jacobson's 1998 book *Whiteness of a Different Color*, which traces what Jacobson calls "a political history of whiteness" through three periods. The argument the framing reading takes from Jacobson is that:

a. American whiteness is a politically constructed and historically shifting racial category, not a stable biological one: in the period 1840 to 1924 the dominant scientific racism divided Europeans into competing "races" (Anglo-Saxon, Celtic, Mediterranean, Slavic, Hebrew) arranged in a hierarchy with Anglo-Saxons on top, and only after the 1924 National Origins Act did those various European "races" gradually reconsolidate into the single "Caucasian" category twentieth-century Americans came to take for granted.
b. American whiteness has been a stable racial category since the country's founding and has not changed in any significant way across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
c. American whiteness is primarily a religious rather than a racial category, defined by Protestant Christianity.
d. American whiteness was abolished as a legal category in 1964.

### 2.

The framing reading argued that the postwar federal economic policies of the late 1940s, 1950s, and 1960s (the GI Bill, the Federal Housing Administration mortgage system, the unionized industrial economy) played a specific structural role in the consolidation of European-immigrant communities into a single "white working-class" identity. What did the framing reading say that role was?

a. The federal programs funneled substantial subsidies (housing, education, mortgage credit) into white-only suburban developments and white-majority union shops while explicitly denying the same subsidies to Black families through redlining and other exclusionary mechanisms, so that European-immigrant communities materially became "white" partly through being placed, by federal policy, on the favored side of a deliberately racist sorting that they could now share in even as they remained on the working-class rather than middle-class side of a separate class sorting.
b. The federal programs distributed benefits equally across racial groups and had no structural racial sorting.
c. The federal programs were designed primarily to benefit Black families and excluded European immigrants.
d. The federal programs had no significant effect on housing or economic patterns in the postwar United States.

### 3.

The framing reading covers four threads in the European American popular tradition. They are:

a. The Ulster Scots ballad tradition (running through Appalachia and the folk revival), the Eastern European Jewish songwriting tradition (running from Tin Pan Alley through the Brill Building), the Italian-Irish-Polish urban Catholic working-class tradition (running from immigrant social-club music through the doo-wop scene and into heartland rock), and the synthesizer pioneers (running from Columbia-Princeton through Wendy Carlos and the Moog).
b. Country, jazz, blues, and gospel.
c. East Coast, West Coast, Midwest, and South.
d. Folk, rock, pop, and electronic.

### 4.

The framing reading argued that the four threads, while distinct in their cultural roots, share two structural features. They are:

a. A long American process by which their carriers came to be understood as white, and a sustained and often unequal dialogue with African American music that runs through every thread.
b. A shared language and a shared religion.
c. A common geographic origin in northern Europe and a common arrival date in the United States.
d. A complete musical independence from one another and from African American music.

### 5.

The Track 1 listening guide on Frankie Lymon and the Teenagers' "Why Do Fools Fall in Love" (1956) described the group's membership. The five Teenagers were:

a. Frankie Lymon (Black, lead, age 13 at the recording session), Sherman Garnes (Black, bass), Jimmy Merchant (Black, tenor), Joe Negroni (Puerto Rican, baritone), and Herman Santiago (Puerto Rican, tenor), and the listening guide framed the racially mixed lineup as characteristic of the upper Manhattan street-corner doo-wop scene rather than as a curated commercial decision.
b. An entirely Italian American family band from Brooklyn.
c. An entirely Jewish American songwriting team that recorded under a pseudonym.
d. A Black Baptist gospel quartet from rural Alabama.

### 6.

The Track 2 listening guide on Joan Baez's "Mary Hamilton" (1960) located the song inside what tradition?

a. The Anglo-Scottish-Irish child-ballad tradition (Francis James Child's late-nineteenth-century collection of long-circulating ballads from the British Isles), transmitted to the American South through the Scots-Irish and English migrations to Appalachia, and revived in the late 1950s and early 1960s by the Greenwich Village folk revival that Baez's debut album was a central early statement of.
b. The Latin American corrido tradition Baez's Mexican father brought to the household.
c. The Eastern European Jewish cantorial tradition.
d. The African American gospel tradition.

### 7.

The Track 3 listening guide on the Drifters' "Up on the Roof" (1962) described the Brill Building system that produced the song. What did the listening guide say about the labor division inside that system?

a. A mostly-white, disproportionately Jewish American songwriter cadre (Goffin-King, Mann-Weil, Greenfield-Sedaka, Pomus-Shuman, Leiber-Stoller) wrote songs for hire on weekly salaries inside the building at 1619 Broadway, and those songs were recorded by a mostly-Black vocal-group cadre (the Drifters, the Shirelles, the Coasters) typically on weekly wages, with the writers retaining publishing rights and royalties and the singers receiving little to nothing of the song's eventual earnings.
b. The songwriters and the singers shared royalties equally.
c. The Brill Building was an integrated cooperative with no division of labor between writers and performers.
d. The system operated exclusively in Los Angeles, not in New York.

### 8.

The Track 3 listening guide identified "Up on the Roof" as a Goffin-King composition. According to the listening guide, the song was first recorded by:

a. The Drifters, in 1962, with Rudy Lewis on lead vocal, arranged and conducted by Garry Sherman; the song had been demoed by Gerry Goffin singing with Carole King on piano before the Drifters' session, and it reached number 5 on the Billboard Hot 100 in early 1963.
b. Ben E. King as a solo single.
c. Aretha Franklin on her Atlantic debut.
d. The Coasters as an Atlantic single in 1960.

### 9.

The Track 4 listening guide on Wendy Carlos's *Switched-On Bach* (1968) described the technical conditions under which the album was made. The recording was:

a. Made by Carlos alone over approximately a year of layered overdubs on a custom-built Moog modular synthesizer in her Manhattan apartment, with no live ensemble; the synthesizer was monophonic (it could play only one note at a time), so every voice in every Bach part had to be recorded as a separate overdub, and the engineering ran in dialogue with Robert Moog through direct correspondence and convention meetings rather than through an academic institution.
b. Made by the entire Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center faculty as a collaborative project.
c. Recorded live in concert at Avery Fisher Hall in New York City.
d. Made by Robert Moog without Carlos's involvement.

### 10.

The Track 4 listening guide named the downstream consequence the framing reading also identified: the synthesizer-pioneer thread's most significant impact in popular music came in a specific direction. The listening guide described it as:

a. The downstream adoption of the synthesizer by Black popular music from 1971 forward, naming Stevie Wonder (the run of albums from *Music of My Mind* in 1972 through *Songs in the Key of Life* in 1976), Sly and the Family Stone (*There's a Riot Goin' On*, 1971), Herbie Hancock (the Headhunters' 1973-1976 jazz-funk crossover), Bernie Worrell (Parliament-Funkadelic), and the Detroit techno pioneers (Atkins, May, Saunderson) who built the entire genre on inexpensive Roland and Korg synthesizers in a city whose Black auto-industry workforce was being hollowed out by the same deindustrialization Springsteen would be writing about on Track 5.
b. The downstream use of the synthesizer exclusively in European art music and academic electronic music institutions.
c. The complete commercial failure of the synthesizer after 1970.
d. The replacement of all acoustic instruments by synthesizers in 1970s popular music.

### 11.

The Track 5 listening guide on Bruce Springsteen's "The River" (1980) described the song's unusual relationship to its source. The narrator of the song is:

a. Springsteen's brother-in-law Mickey Shave, narrating Mickey's own marriage to Springsteen's sister Virginia (Ginny) Springsteen, drawn directly from conversations Springsteen had with Mickey in the late 1970s; the song is unusual inside Springsteen's catalog for the directness of its source, since most of the characters on the surrounding albums are composite figures, and the "I" speaking the lyric is identified as a real person inside Springsteen's own family rather than as Springsteen himself or as a clearly fictional character.
b. A composite figure drawn from Springsteen's reading of Steinbeck and Flannery O'Connor.
c. An autobiographical "I" in which the narrator is Springsteen himself.
d. A fictional Vietnam veteran with no specific real-world model.

### 12.

The Track 5 listening guide described the song's relationship to Module 1's Track 4, "I'm So Lonesome I Could Cry" by Hank Williams. The relationship is:

a. Direct lyrical inheritance: Williams's 1950 single "Long Gone Lonesome Blues" carries the image of going down to a river that is dry, an image Springsteen explicitly borrowed for the chorus of "The River" (and has cited as a source in interviews); the borrowing reaches across two of Module 5's threads at once (the Ulster Scots ballad lineage Williams sits inside, and the urban Catholic working-class lineage Springsteen sits inside), with the country songwriting tradition functioning as the connective tissue between them.
b. No relationship; the two songs are unconnected.
c. A direct cover, with Springsteen recording Williams's melody and lyrics unchanged.
d. A purely visual parallel in the album cover art, with no musical or lyrical connection.
