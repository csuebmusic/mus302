## Part 2: Modules script (spoken)

Conversational, prose, no em dashes. Roughly six minutes spoken at a
normal pace.

The course is organized around four cultural traditions in American
popular music, and the dialogues between them. We start with an
orientation module, and then we move through the four traditions one at
a time. That is the whole shape.

Module 1 is Orientation and Methodology. You complete it first, before
anything else. It teaches you how this course wants you to listen, what
counts as evidence when you write about music, and the cultural-roots
framing that holds the rest of the term together. You will work through
four anchor tracks: Sam Cooke's "A Change Is Gonna Come," the Fania
All-Stars with Celia Cruz performing "Quimbara" live in Zaire in 1974,
Sugar Pie DeSanto's "I Don't Wanna Fuss," and Hank Williams's "I'm So
Lonesome I Could Cry." Four tracks, four traditions, four ways into the
course. By the end of Module 1 you will know what a listening guide
does, you will have a working vocabulary for talking about timbre and
texture and form and gesture, and you will have started the conversation
we will keep having for the rest of the term.

Module 2 is African American Foundational Traditions. This module makes
an argument that runs through the course: American popular music is
unthinkable without the Black musical traditions that built it. Blues,
gospel, R&B, soul, funk, hip hop, and the contemporary pop that draws on
all of them are the through-line. The five anchor tracks move from
Bessie Smith in the 1920s to Sister Rosetta Tharpe, to James Brown, to
Grandmaster Flash and the Furious Five's "The Message," and then to
Beyoncé's "Formation." You will see the lineage as a lineage, and you
will see the social and political conditions the music was responding
to, including the Great Migration, civil rights, the urban crises of the
1980s, and the Movement for Black Lives.

Module 3 is Latin Diasporic Traditions. Here we take seriously that
Latin music in the United States is not one thing but many things,
shaped by migration from across the Caribbean and Latin America, and
shaped by the specific neighborhoods and cities in which those musics
took root. The anchor tracks are Tito Puente, Joe Bataan, Santana,
Selena, and Tego Calderón. You will trace mambo and salsa in New York,
the Chicano rock crossover in the Bay Area, Tejano in South Texas, and
reggaeton out of Puerto Rico. You will see how each tradition argues
with the others, and how each one has had to fight for space inside
what gets counted as American popular music.

Module 4 is Asian American Traditions. This module pushes back on the
idea that there is no such thing as Asian American popular music. There
is, and there has been for a long time. The anchor tracks are A Grain
of Sand from 1973, which is one of the founding documents of Asian
American movement music; Jon Jang's composition in response to the 1982
murder of Vincent Chin; the Jets, a Tongan American family band from
the 1980s; the Invisibl Skratch Piklz and the Filipino American
turntablist tradition that came up in the Bay Area; and Mitski, working
inside and against the white indie rock landscape of the 2010s. The
module argues that Asian American popular music has a history, an
aesthetic, and a politics, all of which deserve to be heard.

Module 5 is European American Immigrant and Working-Class Traditions.
This is the module where we get specific about whiteness, because the
broad category "white" hides more than it reveals. The anchor tracks
are Frankie Lymon and the Teenagers, Joan Baez, the Drifters, Wendy
Carlos, and Bruce Springsteen. You will look at the doo-wop era and the
racially mixed teenage audience that drove it. You will look at the
Brill Building system and name what it actually was, which was a
structurally racist arrangement where a mostly-white songwriter cadre
wrote for Black vocal groups and kept most of the rights and most of
the income. You will look at the folk revival, at the early synthesizer
pioneers, and at the recession-era working-class document that "The
River" turned out to be. By the end of the module you will have a much
more specific account of what "European American" actually means in the
music.

Each module is built the same way, so once you have done Module 1 you
will know the rhythm. There is a framing reading that lays out the
historical and cultural argument for the module. There are five
listening guides, one for each anchor track, that walk you through the
recording in detail. There is a discussion prompt on the discussion
board, and there is a low-stakes quiz that checks the work. And across
all five modules, your research project is moving forward in the
background.

One thing to know about the listening guides. They are interactive: any
term you do not recognize is clickable, and clicking it gives you a
plain-language definition. The vocabulary builds across the course. By
the end of Module 5 you will be reading and using terms that would have
looked technical to you in week one. That is the design. Take your time
with them.

A reminder on order. Module 1 is the gate, and you complete it first.
After Module 1, Modules 2 through 5 run one per week, in that order.
The weekly deadlines are on the syllabus and in Canvas. The course is
asynchronous, so you can work whenever in the week makes sense for you,
but the deadlines are real, and the schedule is condensed because it is
a summer term. Pace yourself.

That is the shape of the course. See you in the modules.
