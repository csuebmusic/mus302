# Module 4 Discussion: Pick a Reflective Question

This is the discussion thread for Module 4. The work happens in two stages: an initial post in which you pick one of the five reflective questions at the end of the Module 4 listening guides and respond to it, and two responses to classmates' posts that Canvas will assign you automatically after the initial-post deadline.

## How it works

Each of the five Module 4 listening guides ends with a reflective question. The questions are:

- **Iijima, Miyamoto, and Chin, "We Are the Children" (1973)**: The framing reading argued that the Asian American Movement musicians of the early 1970s worked primarily in the political-music mode of direct address. "We Are the Children" is the founding example. Pick one specific moment in the recording (the line about being "offspring of the concentration camp," the verse-two lines about "watching war movies with the nextdoor neighbor, secretly rooting for the other side," the verse-three shift from present-tense "leave their stamp" to future-tense "will leave their stamp," the way the three voices come together on the chorus, the bongos under one of the verses) and argue what that moment is doing politically as well as musically. Who is it for, who might it have unsettled in 1973 and after, and does the political work depend on the lyrics alone, or on the lyrics being delivered in this specific way by this specific small group of voices and instruments?
- **Jon Jang, "Are You Chinese or Charlie Chan? / East Wind" (1984)**: Pick one specific moment in the recording and argue what it is doing musically and politically at the same time. Some candidates: the moment the piece leaves the 12-bar blues home base for the dissonant freely improvised bridge; the four percussion strikes inside that bridge that Jang composed to represent the four bat strikes that killed Vincent Chin; the way the East Wind Blowers choir responds to Woody Ichiyasu's lead; the rap exchange between Ichiyasu and Bob Matsueda; the return of the blues after the freely improvised passage. The piece is satirical, political, and musically ambitious all at once; pick the moment where you can feel two of those three at the same time, and unpack what is happening.
- **The Jets, "Crush On You" (1986)**: The framing reading argued that one mode of political work in Asian American popular music is "presence": showing up in the cultural picture at all, in venues and chart positions and national imagination that had not had room for the community before. "Crush On You" is the cleanest case in the module of presence detached from representation; the song is about a teenage crush, not about being Tongan or Pacific Islander or LDS. Argue for or against the claim that a teen-pop song about a crush, performed by an immigrant Pacific Islander family band inside the dominant pop production aesthetic of the moment, is politically meaningful. Use specific moments in the recording to support your argument, and address the question of whether presence-without-representation counts as the political work the framing reading describes, or whether something more is required.
- **Invisibl Skratch Piklz, "Vs. Da Klamz Uv Deth" (1996)**: The framing reading argued that one mode of political work in Asian American popular music is the building of long, sustained dialogues with African American musical traditions. The Piklz' recording is the clearest case in the module of dialogue-by-virtuosity: a Filipino American crew taking an African American art form to its formal limit, working inside the form rather than alongside it. Pick one sub-routine in the recording (any thirty-second to two-minute passage) and argue what is happening in that passage that could not happen anywhere else. Some candidates: a moment when the three DJs lock into a polyphonic groove and you can hear the labor division clearly; a moment when one DJ scratches a melodic figure that the other two follow; a moment when the texture thins to a single DJ; a transition into a new sub-routine. The recording is a collective improvisation that is simultaneously a finished composition; pick the moment where you can hear both at once.
- **Mitski, "Your Best American Girl" (2016)**: The song works on at least two registers simultaneously: a first-person love song about being unable to fit into the life of a particular person from a particular background, and a song about being Asian American inside the indie rock space. Mitski has resisted having the second reading made the only or the main reading; reviewers and listeners have repeatedly pulled the song toward it anyway. Pick one moment in the recording (a specific verse line, a specific transition, a specific instrumental gesture) where the two readings co-exist most clearly, and describe what each reading hears in that moment. What is the relationship between Mitski's stated intention and the song's political effect? Can a song mean something its writer did not intend, and if so, who decides what it means?

Pick one. Read the listening guide for the track if you have not already, then write your post in response to that track's reflective question. The full questions live in the listening guides themselves, with more context than the summaries above.

## What a strong post looks like

A strong post does four things:

- It picks one specific track and engages with that track's reflective question directly. Quoting back part of the question is fine; your job is to answer it.
- It rests on at least one specific moment in the recording. Timestamps are great. So is a precise description of what is happening in the moment.
- It uses the vocabulary the readings have given you. Timbre, texture, form, gesture; content, form, presence as the three modes of political work; the framing reading's account of the panethnic category, the dialogue with African American music, and the post-1965 demographic transformation. You do not have to use all of these. You should use the ones that fit your argument.
- It makes a claim someone could disagree with. The reflective questions are designed to invite a position, not a summary.

Two to four paragraphs is the right length. Longer is fine if you have more to say. Shorter is fine if you say it well.

## Responding to classmates

After the initial-post deadline, Canvas will assign you two classmates' posts to respond to. You will not pick them yourself; Canvas's peer-review tool handles assignment automatically. This means you will likely be reading posts on tracks you did not pick, which is part of the design: the thread spreads coverage across all five tracks rather than concentrating on the most popular questions.

Good responses do one or more of these things:

- Engage with the specific moment your classmate pointed to. If you heard it differently, say so. "I heard that moment doing something else, here is what" is welcome.
- Add to the argument by pointing at a moment in the same track that fits or complicates what your classmate said.
- Connect the post to a moment in another Module 4 track or in earlier modules. Cross-track connections are part of the work, especially given the dialogue this module sustains with the African American tradition Module 2 traced.
- Ask a question that pushes the argument further. Not "great post!" but something more like: "you said the four percussion strikes in the Jang bridge are doing the political work; do you think they would still do that work if a listener did not know what they represent, or does the political work depend on the listener knowing?"

## Logistics

- Initial post: due by Sunday, June 21 (the Module 4 deadline)
- Two assigned responses: due by Sunday, June 28
- Length: two to four paragraphs for the initial post, a paragraph or two for each response
- AI use: allowed with disclosure, per the generative AI policy in the syllabus. If you used AI for this post, end your post with a one-sentence acknowledgment ("I used [tool] to [purpose].")

## Outcomes addressed

This discussion supports SLO 2 (communicating critical thoughts and judgments about the creative work of different artists), SLO 3 (cultural connections), and SLO 5 (identifying and discussing forms and influences within and across traditions). It also supports the GE-UD-3 outcome on applying the methodologies of the arts and humanities, and the Diversity Overlay outcomes on describing the histories and views of cultural groups (the Asian American panethnic category and the communities it gathers) and on the intersection of social identities (the Mitski track foregrounds the intersection of race, gender, and genre-space; the Jets track foregrounds race, religion, and immigration status; the Jang track foregrounds the cross-racial solidarity between Asian American and African American musicians around the Vincent Chin case).
