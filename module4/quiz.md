# Module 4 Checkpoint Quiz

A short quiz covering the framing reading and the five anchor tracks of Module 4. Twelve questions, multiple choice, low-stakes. Students may retake the quiz as many times as they want; highest score counts.

The structured version below is for reading. The "PASTE INTO CANVAS" block further down has each question in plain text, ready to paste one at a time when building the Canvas quiz.

## Quiz introduction (paste into the Canvas quiz description box)

Welcome to the Module 4 Checkpoint Quiz. Like the earlier module quizzes, this one is short and low-stakes. It exists to make sure the framing reading and the five anchor tracks have landed before you move on.

A few things to know before you start:

There are 12 multiple-choice questions. Plan for about 15 to 20 minutes, though you can take as long as you need. There is no time limit.

You can retake this quiz as many times as you want. Your highest score is the one that counts. If you miss a question, go back to the relevant reading or listening guide, then come back and try again.

The questions cover three things: the framing reading's vocabulary and arguments (what makes "Asian American" a panethnic category that had to be invented, the long pre-history of Asian musicians in American popular music, the 1965 Hart-Celler Act and the demographic transformation it set in motion, the modes of political work this music does, the sustained dialogue with African American music), specific facts and contexts from the five anchor tracks (Iijima/Miyamoto/Chin, Jang, the Jets, Invisibl Skratch Piklz, Mitski), and the methodology vocabulary from earlier modules applied to the new tracks (timbre, texture, form, gesture, plus the political-work modes of content, form, and presence).

You should complete the framing reading and all five listening guides before taking this quiz. The quiz is the last thing you do in Module 4, after the discussion post.

If something on the quiz seems unclear or you think a question is wrong, email me. I would rather hear about it than have you stuck.

Good luck.

## Questions (structured for reading)

### 1.

The framing reading argued that "Asian American" is a "category that had to be invented." What did it mean by that?

a. The phrase was coined in May 1968 by two graduate students at UC Berkeley as a deliberate political alternative to "Oriental"; before then, the communities the term covers were thought of separately, by themselves and by everyone else.
b. The phrase was created by the US Census Bureau in the 1970s for demographic tracking purposes.
c. The phrase was invented by record labels in the 1980s to market Asian American musicians.
d. The phrase has always existed but only became commonly used in the 1990s.

### 2.

The framing reading described "Asian American" as a panethnic identity. What does panethnic mean, in the way the reading uses it?

a. An identity that gathers multiple ethnic groups (Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, Korean, South Asian, sometimes Pacific Islander) into a shared political category, on the grounds that they face common conditions in the United States and can do common political work.
b. An identity that requires shared language, religion, or homeland.
c. An identity that applies only to people of mixed Asian and non-Asian heritage.
d. An identity that excludes people born outside Asia.

### 3.

The framing reading traced what it called a long pre-history of Asian musicians in American popular music before the term "Asian American" existed. One of its three threads was Filipino musicians on the Pacific. What did the reading say about them?

a. By the 1920s Filipino musicians were the dominant non-white labor force in jazz orchestras in hotels and ocean liners across Asia, learning American repertoire fast and exporting it back; the music historian Frederick Schenker has called them "the unsung Pacific carriers of jazz."
b. Filipino musicians did not enter American popular music in significant numbers until after 1965.
c. Filipino musicians worked exclusively as session players in Hollywood film studios in the 1930s.
d. Filipino musicians played mainly in Spanish-language operetta companies in the early twentieth century.

### 4.

The 1965 Immigration and Nationality Act (also called the Hart-Celler Act) was, in the framing reading's argument, the single most consequential piece of legal history shaping the modern Asian American population. What did the law do, and what was its demographic consequence for Asian Americans?

a. It removed the national-origin quotas that had effectively closed off Asian immigration since 1924, and over the following decades the Asian American population grew from less than one percent of the US population to almost six percent by the 2010s, with the existing Chinese, Japanese, and Filipino communities expanding through family reunification and new communities (Korean, Indian, Pakistani, Vietnamese, Cambodian, Lao, Hmong) arriving in significant numbers for the first time.
b. It tightened immigration restrictions on Asian people and reduced the existing Asian American population.
c. It applied only to refugees from Southeast Asia after the Vietnam War.
d. It was a symbolic correction with no measurable demographic consequence.

### 5.

The framing reading named three modes by which a song can do political work, the same three modes Module 2's framing reading introduced. They are:

a. Direct address (a song that says, in lyrics, what it thinks about a political situation), formal innovation (a song that reorganizes musical material in a way that disrupts existing hierarchies), and presence (a song whose political work is partly that the artist is in the room, claiming space).
b. Lyrics, melody, and rhythm.
c. Protest, celebration, and lament.
d. Verse, chorus, and bridge.

### 6.

The Track 1 listening guide described "We Are the Children" (1973) as the founding example of Asian American Movement protest music, recorded by three musicians in their twenties for Paredon Records. The trio was:

a. Chris Iijima (a Sansei Japanese American from Manhattan's Upper West Side), Nobuko Miyamoto (a Japanese American singer-dancer who had performed in West Side Story and the Broadway production of Flower Drum Song), and "Charlie" Chin (a Chinese American multi-instrumentalist of Cantonese and Trinidadian Chinese heritage from Queens).
b. Three Chinese American musicians from San Francisco's Chinatown.
c. Three Filipino American musicians from the Bay Area mobile DJ scene.
d. Three Japanese American jazz musicians from Los Angeles.

### 7.

The Track 1 listening guide pointed to the line "We are the offspring of the concentration camp" and noted, citing the Densho Encyclopedia, that the line is significant in the history of American popular music because:

a. It is likely the first English-language song to explicitly name the wartime incarceration of Japanese Americans under Executive Order 9066, recorded twenty-eight years after the camps closed and fifteen years before the Civil Liberties Act of 1988 granted federal reparations.
b. It is the first song to mention the Vincent Chin murder.
c. It is the first song recorded by a Japanese American musician for a major commercial label.
d. It is the only line in the song that the Smithsonian Folkways reissue altered from the original 1973 release.

### 8.

The Track 2 listening guide on Jon Jang's "Are You Chinese or Charlie Chan? / East Wind" (1984) described the piece as a fifteen-minute composition built around two contrasting musical worlds. Its home base form is one of the most stable forms in American popular music, the same form students met in "St. Louis Blues" and "Strange Things Happening Every Day." It is:

a. A 12-bar blues, which Jang chose deliberately so that the political content of the lyrics about the Vincent Chin murder could ride on a familiar African American song form, with the bouncing boogie-woogie piano accompaniment setting the seriousness of the political content against a musical form built for entertainment.
b. A 32-bar AABA Tin Pan Alley standard.
c. A through-composed jazz suite with no recurring form.
d. A simple verse-chorus pop song.

### 9.

The Track 2 listening guide identified four percussion strikes that occur inside the piece's freely improvised dissonant bridge. According to Jang's account to the musicologist Loren Kajikawa, the strikes are:

a. Composed to represent the four times Vincent Chin was struck with the baseball bat that killed him in the McDonald's parking lot in Highland Park, Detroit, on the night of June 19, 1982.
b. A standard salsa percussion pattern transcribed from a Tito Puente recording.
c. A reference to the four-on-the-floor disco beat that dominated the late 1970s.
d. Improvised differently in every performance and not composed at all.

### 10.

The Track 3 listening guide on The Jets' "Crush On You" (1986) described the band as eight of the seventeen children of Maikeli and Vaké Wolfgramm, who immigrated to the United States in 1965. The framing reading's panethnic category was complicated, in this track, by the fact that the Jets are:

a. Tongan American, a Pacific Islander community whose presence inside the AAPI category is contested because Pacific Islander communities are Indigenous to lands the United States colonized (Hawaii in 1898, Samoa in 1899, Guam in 1898), making the relationship to the United States categorically different from voluntary or forced immigration.
b. The first Asian American family band signed to Motown Records.
c. A second-generation Filipino American family from Daly City.
d. A Japanese American family band from Los Angeles.

### 11.

The Track 4 listening guide on Invisibl Skratch Piklz' "Vs. Da Klamz Uv Deth" (1996) described the crew's central technical innovation, the contribution that turntablism scholars (especially Oliver Wang) name as their signature. It is:

a. The team-DJ format in which three turntablists divide the conventional band's labor across three turntables, with one DJ functioning as the drummer (scratching a percussion sample), one as the bassist (manipulating a held low-pitched figure), and one as the soloist (scratching a melodic figure or vocal sample), with the roles rotating across the routine.
b. The invention of the crossfader on the DJ mixer.
c. The first use of digital sampling in a turntable performance.
d. The replacement of vinyl records with CD turntables.

### 12.

The Track 5 listening guide on Mitski's "Your Best American Girl" (2016) argued that the song works on at least two registers simultaneously, and that the song does not require the listener to choose between them. The two readings are:

a. A first-person love song about being unable to fit into the life of a particular person from a particular background, and a song about being Asian American inside the largely white indie rock institutional space; Mitski herself supplied language for both readings in different interviews and Facebook posts.
b. A political protest song and a religious hymn.
c. A break-up song and a celebration of independence.
d. A traditional folk song and a contemporary pop remake.
